Installed figures use static projections. To enable 3D rotation and layout selection, render with options(grip.vignette.interactive = TRUE) and ivue and rgl installed.

metric.mds() fits Euclidean coordinates to graph shortest-path distances. The default backend='sgd' moves pairs of vertices in shuffled order, following Zheng, Pawar and Goodman (2018), with a selectable learning-rate schedule. Legacy graph calls default to the hybrid schedule adapted from the implementation of Hangan, Kobourov and Miller (2026); prepare.sparse.mds() inputs default to Zheng’s weight-dependent fixed-budget exponential schedule (scheduler="zheng"). backend='smacof' updates the full configuration using the optional smacof package. Its majorization steps are designed to reduce total stress; individual SGD pair adjustments need not.

This guide illustrates the differences using existing saved synthetic examples. For a broader comparison of the methods, see Hangan, Kobourov and Miller (2026). The complete experiment retains all 420 original fits, the separate 60-fit weighting comparison, failed outcomes, numerical results, and reproduction instructions.

For higher-dimensional fitting followed by PCA and optional free 3D refinement, see Higher-dimensional metric MDS for 3D visualization.

To record and animate actual SGD pair updates, see Animating metric MDS: paraboloid and saddle.

Fit a graph with either backend

g <- suitesparse.graph('dwt_307')
sgd <- metric.mds(edges=g$edges,n=g$n,dim=3,seed=1,
                  init='random',max.iter=100,diagnostics=FALSE)
smacof.fit <- metric.mds(edges=g$edges,n=g$n,dim=3,seed=1,
                  init='random',max.iter=100,diagnostics=FALSE,
                  backend='smacof',eps=1e-8)

This is a usage recipe, not a new benchmark on dwt_307. Both backends support uniform and inverse-squared stress weights. The default full mode uses uniform weights. An SGD pass and a SMACOF iteration are different amounts of work; compare achieved error and elapsed time, not iteration counts alone. SGD’s schedule completion reports iteration_limit, which does not certify convergence. The hybrid tuning defaults remain provisional calibration choices. The saved backend comparisons do not isolate the learning-rate schedule and therefore do not establish that hybrid is better than "zheng". Hangan et al.’s fixed-switch rationale concerns the usual uniform-weight MDS setting; it is not evidence of superiority for inverse-squared regional sparse stress. See ?metric.mds, under “Learning-rate schedules and evidence,” for the provenance, precise defaults and published-schedule controls.

Two distance targets on the same saddle

The saved experiment uses 64- and 144-point clouds, five paired random starts, and separate allowances of 10, 30, or 100 passes/iterations. Both methods receive the same starting coordinates within each pair. Here we show the 144-point saddle at seed 1 and allowance 100, with uniform stress weights. These displayed runs were selected in advance, not chosen for their appearance.

Ambient distances are straight-line distances between generating points; an exact 3D realization exists. Local graph paths sum edge lengths along a finite grid with horizontal, vertical, and diagonal edges. They are not exact geodesics on the continuous saddle, and their best 3D drawing need not reproduce the generating surface.

Gray shows the generating reference; blue is SGD and orange is SMACOF. The ambient case uses matched-vertex alignment. The local-graph case uses surface-shape alignment: translation, rotation, and reflection match nearby surface locations without requiring vertex labels to agree. Neither alignment resizes or deforms the layouts. Scores use the original returned coordinates.

Normalized error is the square root of raw stress divided by the sum of squared target distances; lower is better. Each point is a separate fit restarted with its own iteration allowance, not a checkpoint along one run. Fitting times exclude shared graph preparation, alignment, and rendering.

The effect of stress weighting

Uniform weighting minimizes squared errors in distance units. The paper’s inverse-squared weighting minimizes squared relative distance errors:

\[ \sum_{i<j} \frac{(\lVert z_i-z_j\rVert-d_{ij})^2}{d_{ij}^2}. \]

An error of 0.2 on a target distance of 1 then counts as much as an error of 2 on a target of 10. Edge lengths determine the target distances; pair.weights determines their influence on the objective.

fit <- metric.mds(edges=g$edges,n=g$n,dim=3,
  pair.weights='inverse_squared',backend='sgd',seed=1)
